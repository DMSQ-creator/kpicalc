What is CAC?

Customer acquisition cost (CAC) is the total cost of winning one new customer. It answers the most basic growth question: how much do we pay for each customer? SaaS companies, agencies and ecommerce brands track it because it is the denominator of unit economics — every pricing, budget and scaling decision eventually runs through it.

CAC = Total sales & marketing spend ÷ New customers

What counts as sales & marketing spend?

More than just ad spend. Fully-loaded CAC includes:

  • Ad spend — paid search, social, display, sponsorships.
  • Salaries and benefits — the sales team, growth marketers, SDRs. This is usually the biggest line item and the one most often left out.
  • Tools and software — CRM, marketing automation, ad platforms, analytics.
  • Agency and contractor fees — plus creative production for campaigns.

Measure both over the same period — typically a month or a quarter — so the ratio is meaningful.

Worked example

You spend $50,000 on sales and marketing in a quarter and win 200 new customers. CAC is $50,000 ÷ 200 = $250 per customer. If each customer generates $30 of gross profit per month, the payback period is $250 ÷ $30 = 8.3 months — each customer has repaid their acquisition cost before the end of month nine.

Payback period (months) = CAC ÷ Monthly gross profit per customer

Common mistakes

  • Mixing up blended and paid CAC. Dividing total spend by all new customers (blended) always looks cheaper than dividing ad spend by ad-acquired customers only (paid). Use blended for the P&L and paid when deciding whether to scale a channel.
  • Ignoring salaries. A team of five on $120k salaries adds $150k per quarter to the numerator. Leave them out and your CAC is fiction.
  • Measuring over too-short periods. A one-week spike in signups after a launch makes CAC look brilliant. Use a month or quarter so acquisition spend and its results land in the same window.
  • Judging CAC without LTV. $250 CAC is fine for a $900-lifetime-value customer and fatal for a $100 one. Pair CAC with our payback check above and always read it against lifetime value.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good CAC?

There is no universal number — it depends on what each customer is worth. The classic benchmark for subscription businesses is an LTV:CAC ratio of at least 3:1: lifetime value should be three times the acquisition cost. A $250 CAC is fine for a $900 LTV customer and fatal for a $100 one. Always judge CAC against your customer's lifetime value, not someone else's number.

What is the difference between blended CAC and paid CAC?

Blended CAC divides all sales and marketing spend by all new customers, including organic and referral signups. Paid CAC divides only paid media spend by the customers that media acquired. Blended CAC shows the true cost of growth; paid CAC tells you what your next customer from ads actually costs. Track both — blended for the P&L, paid for scaling decisions.

Should sales and marketing salaries count in CAC?

Yes. Fully-loaded CAC includes salaries and benefits for sales and marketing staff, tools and software, agency and contractor fees, and ad spend — everything you paid to acquire customers in the period. Leaving out salaries is the most common way CAC gets understated and makes paid channels look more profitable than they are.

What is the difference between CAC and CPA?

CPA (cost per acquisition) usually measures the cost of a single conversion event — a signup, download or lead — on one channel, and often counts media spend only. CAC measures the fully-loaded cost of acquiring a paying customer across the whole sales and marketing effort. CPA is a campaign metric; CAC is a business metric.

How does CAC relate to LTV?

LTV (lifetime value) is what a customer pays you over their whole relationship; CAC is what you paid to win them. The business model works when LTV comfortably exceeds CAC — the common target is a 3:1 LTV:CAC ratio, with a payback period of 12 months or less. High CAC can be fine if retention and monetization make LTV high enough.
